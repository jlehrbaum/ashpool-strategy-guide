Start Here: What Every Player Should Know

Wing Fighter looks like a game about collecting stronger equipment and increasing your Battle Power (BP). But as you progress, you'll discover that how you build matters just as much as how much you build.

A player with lower BP can beat someone much stronger by investing in the right stats, combining equipment effectively, and exploiting weaknesses in their opponent's setup.

This guide explains how to do that. It's a deep dive into the mechanics, strategies, and investment decisions that make the biggest difference in both PVE (fighting the game) and PVP (fighting other players). If you're new to these systems, the eight principles below are the most important things to understand before diving into the detailed sections.

1. BP isn't everything

Higher Battle Power doesn't guarantee victory. A well-optimized 30M player can beat a poorly optimized 45M player. Your stats, equipment combinations, and ability to counter an opponent often matter more than the number next to your name.

2. Every build has a counter

There is no single best build for every situation.

  • Crit is effective against opponents with low Crit Resistance.
  • Dodge is effective against opponents with low Hit Rate.
  • Burn bypasses Dodge, making it useful against dodge-heavy opponents.

Your goal isn't to maximize every stat. It's to strengthen the stats your opponent has the hardest time countering.

3. Pilots are your foundation

Your pilots determine four critical stats: Physical Defense Break (PDB), Energy Defense Break (EDB), Physical Defense (PD), and Energy Defense (ED). These influence how much damage you deal and how much you receive. If they fall significantly behind your opponents, even expensive equipment upgrades may not be enough to compensate.

The takeaway: Don't neglect pilots while chasing new equipment. Build strong offense and keep both defensive stats competitive.

4. Your systems work together

The game's newer systems add different ways to strengthen and specialize your builds:

  • Chip Formations: Create different stat combinations for different battles.
  • Sentinels: Provide powerful abilities and passive bonuses.
  • Mech Spirits: Add combat abilities, stats, and collection bonuses.
  • Engines: Provide another configurable stat boost.

The real power comes from how these systems reinforce one another. A defensive build might combine Dodge, healing bonuses, and damage reduction. A Crit build might combine Crit Rate, Hit Rate, and Crit Damage.

5. Know the difference between raw stats and effective stats

Many of the game's most important stats have caps, but those caps apply after your opponent's counters. For example, Burn Enhancement has a maximum effective value of 100%:

  • In PVE, 100% Burn Enhancement reaches that cap.
  • In PVP, an opponent with 100% Burn Reduction would require you to have 200% Burn Enhancement to reach the same 100% effective value.

Crit and Dodge work similarly, with 80% effective caps in PVP.

The takeaway: A stat above its advertised cap isn't necessarily wasted. What matters is how much remains after your opponent counters it.

6. Focus your investments

Resources are limited, especially if you're free-to-play (F2P) or a light spender. Trying to build everything at once usually leaves you with several mediocre builds instead of one or two strong ones. A few practical starting points:

  • Build a solid PVE Burn setup to improve your performance against bosses.
  • Develop a PVP offense or defense that fits your preferred approach.
  • Max accessible drones and sentinels before chasing expensive, higher-rarity alternatives.
  • Invest in mech combination bonuses because their benefits extend beyond the mechs you equip.

7. Your strategy should change as you progress

Some upgrades do more than increase a number. They unlock entirely new ways to build. One of the biggest milestones is reaching 100% native Burn Enhancement, meaning you hit 100% without using Burn chips. Before that point, you'll generally need a dedicated PVE Burn formation. Afterward, your Crit/Hit formation can double as your PVE Burn formation, freeing another formation for a specialized PVP Burn setup.

Other important milestones include leveling Evil Eye to improve Crit-to-Burn-Crit conversion, maxing key sentinels, and strengthening your pilot stats.

8. Learn why a build works before copying it

This guide includes recommended builds, equipment combinations, stat targets, and investment paths. They're useful starting points, but they aren't universal answers. A build that works brilliantly for one player may underperform for another because their pilots, drones, available resources, or opponents are different.

Try to understand the role of each component. Is it increasing your damage, helping you survive, overcoming an opponent's counter, or strengthening another part of the build?

Part 1: Understanding the Fundamentals

Part 1 is about the thinking behind strong builds, before any specific stat or system. Build Philosophy covers how builds counter each other and where to invest your attention. Pilots covers the foundation stat that everything else sits on top of.

Build Philosophy: How to Think About Builds

Before getting into the mechanics of each system, it's worth stepping back and thinking about how strong builds come together. The game offers a huge number of possible builds, which makes it tempting to optimize everything at once. Resources, especially for f2p and lightly-spending players, are limited. Where you choose to focus matters more than how much you have.

This section lays out the strategic logic behind strong builds. The key ideas: every build has a counter, the layers you optimize matter more than your raw power level, and pairing your primary attributes with the right secondaries is what turns a good build into a winning one.

Every Build Has a Counter

There is no single dominant build. Every offensive style is vulnerable to a defensive style, and every defense has an attacker that can crack it. The strongest players accept this and aim for one of two outcomes:

  • Specialize the secondaries: Pair your primary attribute with the secondaries that enable it. Dodge needs heal to outlast enemies. Crit needs hit (so you can land attacks) and crit damage (so the crits matter). The combinations that win are dodge + heal, or crit + hit + crit damage. A build that stacks one primary attribute without its enablers is weak.
  • Overpower the primaries: Push your primary attribute high enough that the opponent's secondary defenses can't keep up. This is the brute-force path and tends to require deeper investment, but it's effective when specialization isn't an option.

The Layer Hierarchy

When you're deciding where to spend pilot points, chips, or recast materials, this hierarchy is a useful guide. The layers are listed in order of impact, investing well in a higher layer beats investing more in a lower one.

The bottom-of-the-list status of base stats is counterintuitive, it's where the visible numbers live, and it's the easiest thing to track. But it's also the most easily overcome by an opponent who built the upper layers well.

Pairing Primaries with Secondaries

This is where specialization pays off in practice. A few examples:

  • Crit builds need crit resistance or hit rate, depending on the matchup. Against an opponent stacking dodge, you need hit rate to land your crits. Against an opponent stacking crit themselves, you need crit resistance to deny theirs. Then layer crit damage (passive sources) and damage increase (mechs) on top, fights end faster, which naturally counters healing-focused opponents.
  • Dodge builds need heal. Dodge alone slows fights down; heal is what keeps you alive through the hits that do land. The Gridstar + Ladybug + Berserk Dragon + Aqua Spirit defensive build in Part 2 is a clean example.
  • Burn builds (PVE) need crit (up to the Evil Eye ceiling) and crit damage. Once you're at the 100% burn cap, crit is the multiplier that pushes damage further, and Evil Eye routes overflow into burn crit, making crit damage do double duty.

On BP (Battle Power)

BP is the most visible stat in the game and the most misleading. It's useful as a rough screening signal, if someone's BP is dramatically higher than yours, they probably have a build that can challenge you, but it doesn't tell you whether they will beat you. A 30M BP player with a well-tuned consistency-and-scaling build can beat a 45M BP player whose investment is spread thin or focused on base stats. The same player can lose to a 25M BP opponent who happens to counter their setup.

Put simply: BP tells you how much someone has built. It doesn't tell you how well they've built it, or whether their build counters yours.

F2P Realities

If you're not spending heavily, you won't get to fully specialize every build. The options most worth playing with, high-tier sentinels, deep mech combination bonuses, recast materials for chasing gold passives, are the slowest to accumulate f2p, and that's by design.

The practical implication: pick one or two builds you use and concentrate your resources there. A defensive PVP build and a PVE burn build is a reasonable starting pair. Don't try to fully tune three or four builds in parallel, you'll end up with all of them slightly underbuilt and none of them able to beat their natural counters. The chip formation system already pushes you toward this (three saved formations), and treating those slots as your build commitments rather than experiments is the f2p-friendly way to use them.

Pilots: The Foundation

Pilots are the foundation everything else sits on. The Build Philosophy section above puts base stats at the bottom of the layer hierarchy; pilots are the opposite. They drive four core combat stats: Physical Defense Break (PDB), Energy Defense Break (EDB), Physical Defense (PD), and Energy Defense (ED). Together these determine how much damage you deal and absorb. A player who has poured resources into chips, sentinels, and mechs but skipped pilots will have a high BP and a hollow foundation, and will lose to comparably-resourced opponents who built pilots properly.

The Four Core Stats

Pilots affect both sides of the damage equation. You need to think about offense and defense as a paired system, not as separate priorities.

Why Pilots Matter More Than They Look

There are two practical rules worth internalizing:

  • You need to be close enough on these stats to compete. If your offense (PDB/EDB) and defense (PD/ED) are roughly comparable to opponents at your BP, you are competitive, and from there you can win by exploiting an advantage in chips, drones, sentinels, mechs, or passives. If your pilots lag, you have to find a much larger exploitable gap elsewhere to overcome the deficit, and against a well-built opponent that gap may not exist.
  • Strong pilots make you durable beyond your BP. A player with mediocre gear but well-developed pilots is unusually tough for their BP. Pilots scale damage and damage taken in a way that compounds with everything else, so investment here pays off across every build you might run. If you were starting fresh, focusing on pilots before anything else would be defensible, and arguably optimal.

The Equipped Pilot Multiplier

Community One quirk that significantly changes pilot prioritization: the pilot you equip contributes its PDB/EDB/PD/ED at 4x compared to idle pilots. Idle pilots in your roster still contribute, but your equipped pilot's stats dominate the total. Practical implication: when deciding which pilot to level highest, favor the one you equip for the match type you play most, since that pilot's stat line is being multiplied disproportionately. Rotate equipped pilots to match the matchup (physical vs. energy opponents) when the stakes justify it.

Don't Specialize Defensively

This is the most common pilot-investment mistake. If you're a primarily physical-damage player, it's tempting to max your PDB on offense and your PD on defense, while letting EDB and ED lag. Don't. You face both physical and energy attackers in PVP, and an opponent running an energy build will tear through a defender who has poured everything into PD and ignored ED.

The rule is simple: regardless of your offensive specialization, max both PD and ED on defense. A physical player still needs strong ED. An energy player still needs strong PD. Your offensive choice can be specialized, but your defense should be balanced, you don't get to pick what your opponent throws at you.

On offense, specializing is more defensible since you're choosing your own build, but maxing both PDB and EDB is still the long-term goal because some PVE encounters and some PVP matchups reward the flexibility.

Defense Break vs. Defense: How the Math Works

Defense Break (your offensive stat) is compared against the opponent's Defense (their defensive stat). The difference between the two, not the raw values, drives a damage multiplier on top of everything else you've built. The same math applies in reverse on incoming damage: opponent's PDB vs. your PD, and opponent's EDB vs. your ED.

The Formula

If your Break ≥ enemy Defense (neutral or bonus zone):

Damage Multiplier = 1 + ((Your Break − Enemy Defense) / 1000)

If your Break < enemy Defense (penalty zone):

Damage Multiplier = 1 / (1 + ((Enemy Defense − Your Break) / 1000))

The only thing that matters is the gap: Your Break minus Enemy Defense. That gap places you in one of three zones: below (penalty), equal (neutral, 1.00x), or above (bonus). The same logic applies on the defensive side: when your PD exceeds an opponent's PDB, they are in their penalty zone against you.

The Breakpoint

The breakpoint is simple: Enemy Defense. Get to or above that number and the penalty zone disappears entirely. Below it, you are paying a multiplicative tax on every hit.

Why the Asymmetry Matters

The two formulas are not symmetric. Being above gives you a linear bonus; being below gives you an inverted penalty that compounds quickly. Worked examples at a few gap sizes:

GapAbove (Bonus)Below (Penalty)Effective Asymmetry
±1001.10x (+10%)1 / 1.10 = 0.909x (−9.1%)Small gaps are nearly symmetric
±2001.20x (+20%)1 / 1.20 = 0.833x (−16.7%)Gap starts to open
±5001.50x (+50%)1 / 1.50 = 0.667x (−33.3%)Penalty zone is meaningfully worse
±10002.00x (+100%)1 / 2.00 = 0.500x (−50%)Falling 1000 behind halves your damage

The practical implication: the worst place to be is in the penalty zone. The first job of pilot investment is to clear the breakpoint against the opponents you fight, once you are even or slightly above, you are paying no tax and your other systems get to work at full effectiveness. Pushing far above the breakpoint is nice but has diminishing returns relative to lifting yourself out of the penalty zone in the first place.

Worked Example: Even Match

Your Break = 1000, Enemy Defense = 1000 -> Multiplier = 1.00. You deal 100% of your base damage: no bonus, no penalty. This is the floor you want to hit before optimizing anywhere else.

PVP Burn Build

This is my current main PVP build. It regularly beats opponents 10M BP ahead and sometimes 15M+ BP ahead, which is why I now treat it as a build in its own right alongside Defensive and Offensive rather than as a sub-tactic. The reason it holds up against both of the other PVP archetypes:

  • Burn beats dodge. Once burn stacks are applied, they tick regardless of whether subsequent attacks land. Dodge opponents can duck your direct hits but can't dodge burn ticks already applied to them.
  • Burn also pressures crit opponents. Burn applies to crit builds the same way it applies to dodge builds, stacks keep ticking regardless of their defense. Paired with the Crit Resistance chip secondary (which denies their crit on you), you're damaging them while they're struggling to damage you.
  • Crit Resistance covers your own weakness. The secondary makes you a bad target for the dominant crit archetype at the same time your burn is doing offensive work against them.

With a single formation, you're pressuring both of the other main PVP archetypes and absorbing their most dangerous output. The build's weakness is a true heal-stacked opponent with sentinel-based sustain. Fights go on long enough that healing output can keep up with burn ticks, but you have a situational Rendclaw/heal-reduction swap for that matchup.

Burn in PVP (Mechanics Deep-Dive)

A few mechanics worth understanding before you commit to burn as your PVP primary.

Burn damage is not affected by dodge. Once stacks are applied, the damage ticks regardless of whether subsequent attacks land. Burn also scales with fight duration, exactly the kind of fight a dodge/heal opponent wants to extend. This is why burn is specifically a dodge-build counter.

The 100% burn cap in PVP is net of opponent burn decrease. If your opponent runs 50% burn reduce (from a Burn Decrease engine, sentinel passives, or mech sources), you need raw burn enhancement around 150% to deliver 100% net burn damage. Unlike PVE, there's no stopping point in PVP; keep stacking burn chips to overcome opponent burn-decrease.

The anti-burn counter is itself contested. Two defensive strategies exist against a burn opponent:

  • Dodge / Burn Resistance (my preferred counter). You dodge the regular hits, they can't crit you, and you negate the burn ticks with high burn resistance. This covers all three of their damage sources at once.
  • Burn / Burn Decrease (Method's preferred counter). Reasoning: burn builds have their own sustain, dodge drags the fight long, and the longer the fight the more time Star Envoy stacks have to build on them anyway. Burn itself can't be dodged, so a mirror-ish burn pressure plus burn decrease is more direct.

The data isn't settled; the second option has adherents I respect, and the matchup likely depends on BP range and sentinel setup. Running Dodge / Burn Resistance is my own current practice, but if you're getting beaten by a specific burner the mirror approach is worth trying.

Defensively, if you run a dodge or heal build and don't cover burn, you lose to burn. Burn ticks straight through dodge, and in long fights burn climbs to meaningful values. Burn Resistance (chips or engine) is the defensive piece a dodge or heal build cannot skip.

Understanding Your Tools

Chip Formations let you save up to three formations of stat-boosting chips. You assign chips to tailor your stats (burn, crit, dodge, hit rate, etc.) for different scenarios. Key constraints include the 100% cap on drone type enhancements (burning, electric, reflect) net of opponent resistance, and the 80% net cap on dodge and crit rate against players. In PVE, crit rate is capped at 80% as well, but the Evil Eye mech spirit allows you to convert excess crit into burn crit, which effectively extends the useful ceiling. See the Chip Formations section for details.

Sentinels are companion units (similar to drones) with up to four passive abilities at four stars. Passives come in three rarity tiers (gold, purple, and blue) and scale with star level, so a maxed low-rarity sentinel often outperforms a high-rarity sentinel at low stars. You equip one sentinel at a time. Unlike drone passives, sentinel passives stack across slots, meaning the same passive type appearing in multiple slots adds up. Note that you can't get two golds of the same passive on a single sentinel, but you can get a gold plus a purple plus a blue of the same type, which still produces a meaningful stacked bonus once the sentinel is fully leveled.

Mech Spirits provide a core skill and up to four attribute bonuses. You equip three mech spirits at a time and should swap them based on the situation. Beyond what you equip, your entire mech collection contributes passive bonuses based on the four attributes. In addition, you can get combination bonuses by accumulating and leveling mech spirits, there are 14 combinations with up to 22 levels each. You can advance the combination level when each mech in the combination has achieved a specified level itself. For example, to get to level 22 of the combination bonus, each of the relevant mechs must be at level 60.

Mech spirits have the most room to grow of any system covered in this guide. Especially early on, you can accumulate a tremendous amount of attribute bonus just by leveling mechs. The 14 combination bonuses extend that runway considerably beyond the mechs you personally equip.

Engines provide a stat boost that you select before battle. Each engine has a base bonus that grows as you level it, and the bonus doubles when equipped. For example, a grade 7 hit rate engine gives 35% hit rate passively and 70% when equipped.

How to Build Around a Strategy

A strong build layers all of these systems together to hit your caps efficiently. For a PVE burn build, you combine burn chips, mech combination bonuses (e.g., 22% passive burn from mechs alone), sentinel passives, and possibly Magic Armor to reach the 100% burn cap, then redirect everything else into crit, but only up to the ceiling that Evil Eye can absorb (more on this below). For PVP, remember that caps like 80% dodge are net values after your opponent's resistance. If your dodge is 160% and your opponent has 100% hit rate, your effective dodge is only 60%, so you still have room to grow. This means stacking dodge beyond 80% is not wasted if opponents have high hit rate. The same logic applies to crit rate in PVP.

Chip Formations

Chip formations give you three saved sets of stat chips, each tied to one of your underlying battle formations. You can swap between them before battles, but each set needs to align with the rest of your equipment for that formation (gems, gears, drones, sentinels, etc.). Most players will dedicate one formation to PVE and the other two to PVP.

The Evil Eye Conversion Ceiling (PVE)

This is the most important nuance for PVE burn builds.

Mechanic Crit rate in PVE is capped at 80% for direct weapon crits, same as PVP. Evil Eye runs on a parallel track: it converts your full crit rate (net of enemy crit resistance) into burn crit rate at a fraction of the original value. Since PVE bosses appear to have no crit resistance, your full raw crit rate feeds the conversion. The 80% cap applies only to weapon crits, not to the burn crit Evil Eye generates, so every additional point of crit is still producing more burn crit damage even past 80% raw.

Mechanic The conversion rate improves substantially as Evil Eye levels. At Level 1, every 10% raw crit becomes 2% burn crit. At max level, every 10% raw crit becomes 5.6% burn crit. So the raw crit needed to produce the same burn crit drops as Evil Eye levels up.

Hard capCommunity There's also a cap on burn crit itself at ~80%. The 100% columns above are reference points for the raw conversion math; in practice the ceiling you're stacking toward is 80%, so the ~400% / ~143% numbers are the practical places to stop at the two ends of Evil Eye's range.

Practical takeaway: track your actual burn crit stat as your stop point, not raw crit. At low Evil Eye there is a lot of room (keep stacking crit), and at max Evil Eye the ceiling tightens (recheck and redirect past ~143% raw crit toward crit damage, hit rate, or other useful stats).

If you don't run Evil Eye at all, the regular 80% PVE crit cap applies and there's no reason to push crit past that.

Building Toward the Burn Cap

Hard capMechanic The 100% cap on burn enhancement is always a net cap, raw burn minus opponent burn decrease. It never changes. What changes is how much raw burn you need to reach 100% net, depending on the matchup:
  • PVE: Bosses have no burn decrease, so 100% raw burn = 100% effective burn. Hard stop; past 100% raw does nothing.
  • PVP vs. opponent with no burn decrease: Same as PVE, 100% raw = 100% effective. Past 100% does nothing.
  • PVP vs. opponent with 50% burn decrease: Need 150% raw burn to deliver 100% effective. Burn chips past 150% start to over-shoot.
  • PVP vs. opponent with 100% burn decrease: Need 200% raw burn to deliver 100% effective. Still capped at 100% effective, just takes more raw to get there.

In short: PVE investment stops at 100% raw; PVP investment is about overcoming the opponent's reduction, not raising the cap. There's no stat gain for stacking raw burn above (100% + opponent's burn decrease).

For a burn-oriented PVE build, once you hit 100% burn from all sources combined (chips, sentinel passives, mech combination bonuses, equipped mech skills, Camp Tech), redirect remaining chip slots into crit. For PVP, keep stacking burn chips as long as your target opponents are running meaningful burn decrease.

Example from my setup: 22% burn enhancement from mech collection + ~20% from Magic Armor leaves ~60% to reach 100% raw from chips. That's the PVE finish line and the PVP starting line against opponents with burn resistance.

The Canonical Pairings

Over time our legion settled on a short list of chip-pair formations that cover the matchups you face. Mixing stats outside this list spreads your chips too thin and leaves you "average at everything, strong at nothing", the loss pattern that shows up most clearly in Star Scramble.

  • Dodge / Crit Resistance
  • Crit / Hit Rate
  • Burn Enhancement / Crit Resistance
  • Crit / Crit Resistance
  • Dodge / Hit Rate

You only run three of these at a time. The three you pick should match the roles your build needs to cover.

Formation Progression: Before, At, and After 100% Native Burn

The right three formations change meaningfully as you progress. The key milestone is reaching 100% native burn, that is, 100% burn enhancement from mech combination bonuses, sentinel passives, equipped mech skills, and Camp Tech alone, with no burn chips equipped. Hitting that threshold effectively frees up an entire chip formation for PVP specialization, because your crit/hit formation now reaches the PVE burn cap on its own.

Preference The three stages:

  • Stage 1, Below 100% native burn. You need a dedicated PVE burn formation to reach the cap. My three formations at this stage:
    • F1, Crit / Hit Rate: PVP offense
    • F2, Dodge / Crit Resistance: PVP defense
    • F3, Burn / Crit (PVE): burn and crit chips both collecting on this formation to reach 100% burn and push crit for Evil Eye conversion. When burn chips roll Crit Resistance as their secondary stat, hold onto them, they're the ones you'll want on F3 later when it converts to a dedicated PVP burn build.
  • Stage 2, At 100% native burn. Your crit/hit PVP formation now also reaches the PVE burn cap without any burn chips, so one of your three formation slots is freed. F3 becomes available for a dedicated PVP use. Common setup:
    • F1, Crit / Hit Rate: PVP offense (also doubles as your PVE formation now)
    • F2, Dodge / Crit Resistance: PVP defense
    • F3, Burn / Crit Resistance: PVP Burn Build. Burn chips are focused on overcoming opponent burn decrease (no longer needed to hit the PVE cap), with Crit Resistance as the secondary to deny opposing crit builds.
  • Stage 3, Past 100% native burn. Same three formations as Stage 2; your burn chips now stack higher raw burn (150%+ raw) because they're pushing past opponent burn resistance rather than reaching the PVE cap. This is the setup where PVP Burn Build can beat opponents 10M+ BP ahead.

Why not jump to Stage 2 or 3 setup early? Because moving to Stage 2 without 100% native burn means pulling burn chips off your PVE formation. Your PVE output drops, and your PVP burn build is still too weak to carry fights, you end up weaker in both modes. Grind the native burn stack first (mech combos, Camp Tech burn enhancement, sentinel burn passives) and transition only when it's there.

Chip Placement Logic Across Formations

Formation 1 (Crit / Hit) gets first pick on crit chips that roll Hit as a strong secondary. If a crit chip has crit resistance instead (and that's useful on the matchups you expect), it goes on Formation 2 or 3 depending on what's weakest there. Burn chips go on Formation 3; the secondary doesn't matter much for the PVE version, but if the chip rolls crit resistance it's also useful for the anti-dodge PVP version.

Auto Mode Setup in the Chip Factory

The Chip Factory's Auto Mode lets you run continuous chip manufacturing with conditions that pause production when something useful comes out. I configure it separately for each of my three formations.

The key setting is "Stop when chip rating increases," which halts the factory whenever it produces a chip that would be an upgrade over what's currently equipped on the active formation. Combined with the "And" relationship between the two attribute rows, this gets tighter as the factory levels up, set and forget, tighten all the way. Attribute filters per formation:

  • Formation 1 (Crit / Hit): Crit Rate primary, Hit Rate secondary
  • Formation 2 (Dodge / Crit Resistance): Dodge Rate primary, Crit Resistance secondary
  • Formation 3 (Burn / Crit Resistance): Burning Enhancement and Crit Rate as the two primary rows (joined by "Or"); Crit Resistance as the secondary on the Burn row for anti-dodge PVP use

Formation 3 is the only formation where two different primary attributes are both actively useful, you're chasing 100% burn (PVE cap, PVP starting point) and loading up on crit after that for weapon-crit and drone-skill damage. Having both configured as primaries means the factory stops and lets you evaluate whenever it produces an upgrade in either category.

Raise your minimum chip-grade filter as the factory levels (e.g., Iridium 5+ only). Chip tiers surface progressively: Stardust 3, Aether, Quantum. Letting trash tiers through just slows your daily grind.

Dodge vs. Crit: When to Switch

The underlying principle: pick the primary your opponent is least equipped to counter. Hit Rate counters Dodge; Crit Resistance counters Crit. Favor Dodge against opponents with low Hit Rate, and favor Crit against opponents with low Crit Resistance. Both caps in PVP are net values (your stat minus opponent's counter), so raw stacking past 80% still produces returns when the opposing counter is high.

  • Opponent has low Crit Resistance? Favor Crit, their defense can't deny what you're throwing.
  • Opponent has low Hit Rate? Favor Dodge, net dodge stays high, so your chips do real work.
  • Opponent has high Dodge and high Hit Rate (hard to crit, hard to dodge)? Burn is often the right pick, it bypasses dodge entirely.

Separate from the primary pick: make sure your Hit Rate is high enough to land attacks against high-Dodge opponents. Hit Rate is a prerequisite for crit, not a substitute for the primary.

Burn Drones

Burn is one of the most flexible damage types in the game, strong in PVE against bosses, and useful in PVP because it bypasses dodge and scales over long fights (covered in Part 2). Choosing the right burn drones, and progressing them in the right order, is one of the higher-impact decisions you'll make.

Foundation: Two Maxed Ninjas

Most players should start by maxing 1–2 Ninjas. Ninjas are accessible, cheap to level relative to higher-rarity drones, and a maxed Ninja meaningfully outperforms an under-leveled higher-rarity drone (the same star-scaling logic that makes maxed Gridstars beat low-star SSRs). Two maxed Ninjas give you a working burn build today and let you compete in PVE while you decide where to invest next.

The R vs. SR Fork

Once you have your Ninja foundation, you face a choice: add R-tier burn drones or work toward an SR-tier burn drone. These are genuine alternatives, not a sequence, most players pick one path based on how much they plan to invest.

  • R-tier path: Faster to max, lower ceiling. Good for F2P or lightly-spending players who want a competitive burn lineup sooner. R drones include Lich, Scorpion Lion, and Cerberus.
  • SR-tier path: Slower and more expensive, but higher ceiling. SR burners (Sagittarius, Lyra, Fornax) outperform R drones once leveled. The most common F2P long-term path is 2x maxed Ninja + 1 maxed SR. Heavier spenders may stack multiple SRs.

There's no wrong answer between R and SR, it depends on your time horizon and resource budget. The mistake is splitting investment across both tiers, which leaves you with under-leveled drones in both and no maxed lineup.

Choosing Among R-Tier Burn Drones

If you go the R-tier route, the three options are not equal. Lich is meaningfully stronger than Cerberus or Scorpion Lion, and the reasoning is worth understanding because it generalizes to how you should evaluate burn drones in general.

DroneEffectReal Value
Lich+15% to all burn damage, every burn tick, all the timeHighest. The bonus applies to every burn stack, every tick, across every burn drone you run, and is always active. It scales with everything else you stack.
CerberusSkill damage 255% + 3.5% per burn stack (max ~276% at 6 stacks)Modest. Roughly +8% to one drone's skill hit at full burn, not nothing, but it's a one-time bonus on a single drone where Lich is multiplying everything.
Scorpion Lion+1 burn stack when recoil is activeLower than it looks and getting worse over time. Burn caps at 6 stacks and you reach the cap within 2–3 cycles of attacks anyway; past cap the extra stack typically just replaces an old one (FIFO queue) rather than adding new damage. The passive is also conditional on recoil being active, a niche that mattered when recoil drones were common and shrinks further every patch cycle that recoil stays out of the meta.

The general principle: burn drones whose bonuses apply continuously to all burn damage are more valuable than burn drones whose bonuses are conditional, capped, or applied to a single hit. Lich satisfies the first criterion; Cerberus and Scorpion Lion run into the second.

Drones and Crit: What Actually Converts

Community A quirk worth knowing before you plan your crit stack: drone normal attacks do not participate in the global crit system at all. A drone's normal-attack crit rate is set by its Spot Weakness passive, full stop. Your global crit rate doesn't buff it, and the opponent's global crit resistance doesn't dampen it. If a drone has no Spot Weakness passive, that drone never crits on normal attacks, which also means Raging Assault (which multiplies crit damage) is a wasted passive without Spot Weakness paired with it.

Downstream implications:

  • Evil Eye's crit -> burn crit conversion (covered in Chip Formations) likely applies to drone skills and your own weapon crit, but not to drone normal-attack ticks. The practical PVE conclusion is unchanged, stack crit anyway, because it still boosts your weapons and drone skills, but it does mean the burn-tick damage from Lich/Sagittarius flaming status is not amplified by Evil Eye the way weapon crits are.
  • Pair Spot Weakness with Raging Assault on any drone where you expect normal attacks to crit. Running one without the other is wasted investment.

Sentinels

Sentinels offer deep tactical flexibility to exploit specific weaknesses in your opponent's build. Your goal is to build a roster of sentinels with different passive combinations so you can swap between them depending on the fight.

Core Mechanics

Sentinels gain one passive at each star level, up to four passives at four stars. Each time you raise the star level after 4, one of the four passives is enhanced (just like drones). Critically, passives scale with the underlying star level of the sentinel. This means a maxed G-class sentinel (40 stars, achievable in 12 days) will often outperform even a well-rolled SSR that is only at a few stars, because the SSR's passives have not had a chance to scale yet. There are 18 different passive types, and each slot rolls from a pool of three rarity tiers: gold (9% chance), purple (18%), and blue (~73%). The gold rate is dramatically higher than the 0.42% gold rate on drones, which is what makes the rerolling strategy viable (see below).

Note on passives at low stars: Passives on low-star drones and sentinels are not useless, they do function, but the benefit is much lower than at high stars. The real value comes from maxing your sentinel and letting the passives scale fully.

SavagePlate (N-class), Best Offensive Starter: Its key advantage is a 2-second skill cooldown, the fastest available. The only other sentinel with a 2-second skill cooldown is Rendclaw (SSR). Even in extended fights, the 2-second cooldown means you fire your skill slightly more often than sentinels on a 4- or 6-second cycle. Since SavagePlate is meant for offensive builds, you should generally expect shorter fights, but you still benefit from the faster cycle even when things go long. SavagePlate with a gold heal-reduction passive is also the specific counter sentinel against opponents running heal-stacked Gridstars.

SR-class (e.g., Arcglow, Glimshade): Reasonable mid-term targets, maxable in roughly 4–5 months. Personally planning to go with an Arcglow because heal enhancement is a benefit you can only get from sentinels. Doomblast fires every 4 seconds but its healing enhancement (+10%) stacks up to 5 times, giving it strong long-fight value once maxed.

Sentinel Passives and Rerolling

The Reverse Merge Technique

When you find a 1-star sentinel with a desirable gold passive, use it as the base and merge a higher-star sentinel into it. The 1-star keeps its gold passive, and the three other slots reroll. You effectively get a free reroll of three slots. The practical cost of a reverse-merge cycle is low, about 30 chips to buy the fresh 1-star sentinel.

How to do it: First, level down the higher-star sentinel to zero before merging it in, this is critical because leveling down returns overclock chips you'd otherwise burn. Then merge the higher-star sentinel into the 1-star base. You keep the one passive on your base sentinel, lose the four passives on the sentinel you merged in, and gain three new random passives. The merge still progresses the base sentinel toward max stars, so this isn't a free re-roll in a stars sense, you're also spending a sentinel copy's worth of progression. You can repeat the process indefinitely, even after hitting max stars.

Side sentinel strategy: Build one or more side sentinels. Get them to four stars and do a reverse merge as soon as you find a 1-star with your desired passive. Once you prefer the side sentinel's passives, merge your main into it. Low-risk way to chase upgrades without disrupting your current setup. I aim to have around half a dozen of these low 4+ star sentinels ready to go.

Sentinel Passives, Stacking and Rarity

Unlike drone passives, sentinel passives stack across slots: the same passive type appearing in multiple slots adds up. You can't get two golds of the same passive type on one sentinel, but you can have a gold, a purple, and a blue of the same type, and those will stack together for a meaningful combined bonus on a maxed sentinel. With a ~31.5% chance of rolling at least one gold across four slots, the rerolling system is generous enough that you can realistically pursue strong passive combinations at a relatively low cost in chips.

  • Glimshade (SR, hybrid): Gold Sentinel Enhance is the practical top pick (multiplier on the main skill); Gold Heal Enhance is a viable alternate. Requires at least one burn drone equipped for the heal to trigger.
  • Doomblast (SSR, defensive): Gold Heal Enhance, Gold Sentinel Enhance, Purple Heal Enhance, Gold Heal Reduce. Doomblast's active is a sentinel-type heal, so Sentinel Enhance multiplies the heal on top of Heal Enhance, two independent multipliers that compound. The pair also protects you against opponents running Gold Sentinel Reduce, which otherwise neuters the Doomblast heal.
  • Rendclaw (SSR, debuff / neutralizer): Gold Heal Reduce, Purple Heal Reduce, Gold Sentinel Enhance (for the active's own damage), Gold Sentinel Reduce. Do not put Gold Heal Enhance on a Rendclaw, it's a debuff sentinel, not a sustain sentinel.

Sentinel Enhancement is a multiplier on the primary attack/skill number only, not on secondary skills, and the passive is multiplicative, not additive. A gold Sentinel Enhance at full strength takes a 440% skill to 440 × 1.75 = 770%. "Attack" and "HP" passives, by contrast, are flat values. Keep this distinction in mind when evaluating which passives are moving the damage needle.

Sentinel Spending Priorities and Progression Timeline

Short Term (1–2 Months)

Max 2–3 Gridstars with different passive combinations (~12 days of chip accumulations each). Use the reverse merge technique to chase gold passives. Set up your three chip formations. Collect mech spirits and start leveling Berserk Dragon and Ladybug. Buy at least one of most sentinels for the 9 combination bonuses (one-time each). Buy one of each SSR sentinel for their combination bonus.

Max a SavagePlate (~30 days of chip accumulation). Continue refining sentinel passives through merging. Shift pilot spend toward mech spirits, mechs have the most cheap room to grow, and the 14 passive combination bonuses (up to 22 levels each) provide massive returns. Work toward 100% burn cap through chips so you can drop Magic Armor for Dragon Emperor.

Medium to Long Term (4+ Months to 1.5 Years)

Build an SR sentinel like Arcglow or Glimshade (4–5 months). In parallel, begin slowly accumulating SSR sentinel copies (Doomblast or Rendclaw) as a 1.5-year project. Continue leveling mechs toward the level 60 threshold for top-tier combination bonuses.

On SSR Sentinels

The main problem with SSRs is that their passives will be low value for a very long time because passives scale with star level. A gold sentinel enhancement on a 3-star Rendclaw sounds great, but it will take 37 more purchases to max. Meanwhile, a well-rolled maxed Gridstar or SavagePlate is outperforming it right now. SSRs are a long game, invest slowly and do not expect returns for at least a year.

Mech Spirits

Mech spirits have the most room to grow of any system covered in this guide. You equip three at a time, and every mech spirit in your collection contributes to passive combination bonuses whether equipped or not. There are 14 different combinations, each with up to 22 levels. These bonuses can be enormous: for example, the Quantum Shift combination gives 2% dodge at level 1 but scales up to 44% dodge at level 22. Each mech needs to reach a specified level to unlock the next tier of the bonus (with level 60 required for the top tier). Buy lots of mech spirits and level them, this is one of the highest-value investments in the game.

How Mech Leveling Works

Mech spirits follow a repeating 5-level cycle. At levels 1 through 4, you unlock or upgrade one attribute bonus slot each level (one slot at level 1, up to four at level 4). At level 5, the core skill upgrades. The cycle repeats: levels 6–9 upgrade attribute slots, level 10 upgrades the core skill again, and so on.

Mech Investment Strategy

If you already have good pilots, you should seriously consider shifting pilot spend toward mechs, at least early on. There is a lot of cheap room to grow, and between the passive bonuses on each mech as well as the 14 combination bonuses (up to 22 levels each) you can get significant returns.

Note: The Evil Eye and Star Envoy Synergy

SynergyEvil Eye and Star Envoy are the key pair for a serious PVE burn build, and they pair specifically because they convert overflow stats into burn damage through parallel mechanics. Evil Eye routes overflow crit (above the 80% PVE cap) into burn crit. Star Envoy routes overflow burn enhancement (above target burn decrease) into reliable burn damage stacks. In PVE, where bosses have no burn decrease and no crit resistance, both mechs reach maximum effectiveness: every point of burn enhancement above 0 increases Star Envoy's trigger chance, and at 100% burn enhancement you have guaranteed Star Envoy triggers on every third skill. The two effects then compound, Star Envoy's stacking burn damage bonus amplifies the same burn ticks that Evil Eye's burn crits are landing. Running both is what makes PVE crit-burn builds so much stronger than their burn-only counterparts.

Engines

Engines are the simplest of the newer systems but still matter for optimization. You select one engine to equip before each battle. Each engine provides a passive bonus that grows as you level it (each upgrade adds roughly 5% to the base bonus), and the bonus doubles when equipped. For example, a grade 7 hit rate engine gives 35% hit rate passively and 70% when equipped.

Four of the five available engines reduce specific incoming damage types (burn, electric, reflect) or counter crits (crit resistance). The hit rate engine is the odd one out, it's your offensive option, negating opponent dodge so your attacks land. Which engine you pick comes down to the matchup: what are you trying to accomplish, and what's the opponent likely to be running?

Camp Technology

Camp Technology is a separate progression track, but it's one of the highest-impact stat sources in the game and interacts tightly with every build in Parts 1 and 2. It has four tabs: Basic, Advanced, Top Tech, and Super Tech. Top Tech and Super Tech were added in v1.7.800 (April 2026) and are where most of the current build-defining investment goes.

Costs double every ~10 levels. BP inflation is a real risk here: don't try to level every stat because unused stats convert diamonds into BP without producing damage, which hurts your matchmaking. Pick the stats you use and ignore the rest.

Recommended Priority Path

The path I use follows Method's analysis, Final Damage first, then PVP-specific stats, then burn enhancement for burn-build players.

  1. Community Final Damage Increase (FDI) and Final Damage Decrease (FDD). These multipliers apply at the very end of the damage calculation, after everything else, which means they scale all your damage (weapons, drones, mechs) and all your incoming damage reduction. There's a non-linear penalty below 50 FDI, damage drops hard, so getting FDI past 50 is a top priority. For Super Tech, both FDD slots to level 60 first.
  2. PVP Damage and PVP Damage Reduction. Core PVP scaling after FDI/FDD are stable.
  3. Burn Enhancement (Top Tech). If you're pursuing a burn build, this is one of the paths to native 100% burn without consuming chip slots. I currently get about 9% of my burn from Camp Tech.
  4. Hit Rate, Crit Resistance, Dodge, Crit Rate (Top Tech, each to level 10, then cycle). Round out the stat profile after the big three above are in place.

Basic Tech attack boosts work like flat red-gem damage, which means they favor fast-firing weapons. Prioritize MG > WG > Missile for Basic Tech attack upgrades. Basic Tech also contains ~55.5k HP total, worth maxing.

Titan Sets & Weapon Tech

Equipment choices around Titan sets and Legion/Weapon Tech are foundational enough to compete alongside the other investments covered above. Pick your Titan set to match your damage type and the current Titan season, keep 5/5 so the set bonus doesn't drop, and invest in weapon tech in a disciplined order.

Titan Sets

Three sets, each tuned to a specific damage type and Titan rotation:

  • Conqueror (physical). Default physical Titan gear set. Conqueror is also the name of a fighter class. The 5-piece set bonus requires flying the Conqueror fighter and equipping the Conqueror Titan set. When the guide says "Conqueror" in a build table under "Fighter," it means the fighter class; the Titan Sets section here refers to the gear set.
  • Destroyer (energy). The go-to energy set. Superior to Thor in every slot except the MG. Current Kraken Titan season rewards full Destroyer with +100% damage.
  • Thor (energy). Mostly underwhelming, "avoid at all costs" is the rule for most players. One exception: Thor MG performs well in the Arachne Titan season.

The 5/5 Titan set bonus matters more than individual piece stats. Breaking the set to insert normal armor drops a 1.3x adjusted-attack bonus that applies to all three weapons and flows into your drones, sentinels, and mechs. Normal armor can deal damage in PVP battle logs, but giving up the 5/5 bonus for it is almost never worth the trade in Titan Challenge, and usually not worth it in PVP either.

The Titan rotation changes which set is temporarily optimal: Kraken rotations reward Destroyer; Arachne rotations reward Thor. Check the current season before committing shards.

Weapon Tech Priorities

For a full-Destroyer energy PVP build, the Weapon Tech investment order (after Aerial +/- is maxed, see below) is:

  1. MG: base attack -> crit rate -> crit damage -> attack rate
  2. WG: same four, in the same order
  3. Missile: same four, in the same order

Keep MG, WG, Missile, and Shield at similar levels overall. MG and Missile get early priority because MG attack speed and the Inferno missile boost are high-impact.

Aerial +/- is higher priority than any of the above, and the reason is a recent rule change: in patch 1.7.820 (July 2026), the game updated so that attributes increasing damage against aerial/ground/sea/boss enemies now also apply to drones, mech spirits, and sentinels. These stats used to be weapons-only; they now boost your entire damage portfolio in PVE. Tyche's aerial-damage passive, Ame-no-Uzume's aerial reduction, and the Legion Tech aerial slots all benefit from this change. Prioritize both aerial increase and aerial reduction to level 10.

Legion Tech investment priorities for PVP: Sky Eagle Tech, Crystal Prism, and Eternal Pivot to level 80; everything else to level 60.

Prism winggun is the only winggun that auto-targets and fires behind you, making it the right PVE choice if you're not running full Titan gear.

Equipment Management

Downgrade excess gear to Rainbow only, don't melt all the way to gold or red, the lower tiers lose too much value in the melt. Keep a dummy set of extra pieces so overflow doesn't get consumed as blanks during upgrades. Only Titan + fighter set bonuses matter for PVP performance; normal gear pieces (Batarang, etc.) perform the same.

Military Rank is a special case worth calling out: unlike Legion Tech or Top Tech, where leveling unused stats produces BP bloat with no fight impact, Military Rank adds attack and HP that apply in every battle. Treat Rank as competitive BP, not padded BP.

Part 4: Resource & Event Economy

Build strategy is only half the game. The other half is managing a slow trickle of resources (tickets, diamonds, shards, chips) so that when the right event lands you have the stockpile to make the most of it. This section covers the day-to-day routine you need to execute the strategies in Parts 1 and 2.

The Weekly Event Cycle

The game rotates event types on a weekly cadence: Mech week, Pilot week, Sentinel week, Titan week, Drone week. Each event awards tiered rewards for completing 100, 200, 300 purchases during the event window, and those tiered rewards only count if the pulls happen in-event. The currency used varies by event:

  • Mech week: 400 pulls for a full event cycle. Mech tickets are free pulls you've accumulated; you don't buy them. The paid alternative is diamonds: 300 per pull, or 2,600 for 10 pulls.
  • Pilot week: 300 pulls for a full cycle. Same model as mech, pilot tickets are free pulls. Diamonds alternative is also 300 per pull / 2,600 per 10.
  • Drone week: 8,000 Rhenite per round. Rhenite is purchasable in the Resource tab at 1 per 10 diamonds, so a round effectively costs up to 80,000 diamonds if you buy all the way to the cap (but see the drone-event section below, you only pay 20% of that in practice).
  • Sentinel week: 8,000 sentinel chips per round. Same 1 per 10 diamonds in the Resource tab.

The core rule: save event-specific currency for the matching week, since the tiered rewards only pay out during the event.

Don't open selectable pilot or mech chests before the matching event starts, since the opens may count toward event thresholds. Mech week is the biggest sink, more room to grow than any other system in this update, so prioritize mech ticket accumulation.

The Drone-Event Buy/Sell Technique

The highest-impact event-economy move in the game right now. The short version: each round looks like it costs 8,000 Rhenite, but you actually only lose about 1,600. You can run the event up to 4 times a month for a huge payout.

How the 8,000 Rhenite works

Each round asks you to spend 8,000 Rhenite on drones before rewards unlock. The trick: during the event, you can sell any drone right back for 80% of what you paid. Buy an SR drone for 1,000 Rhenite, sell it immediately, get 800 back, net cost 200. Keep buying and selling until you've hit 8,000 Rhenite in total purchases, and your real cost for the round is just 1,600 Rhenite (20% of 8,000). Buy and sell as fast as possible.

Modified chips

Chips are an alternative way to pay toward the 8,000, with each chip counting as 2 Rhenite. You spend them by recasting a drone (re-rolling to get new passives). You should only recast during this event, since it's one of the best chip sinks in the game. The catch: chips don't get refunded. Once spent, they're gone.

Running out of Rhenite?

If you can't cover 4 rounds with Rhenite and chips, you can buy Rhenite with diamonds at 10 diamonds per 1 Rhenite. Pricey, but the event pays out so many diamonds that it's often still worth it.

Rewards

Every round pays 5,800 diamonds, 800 chips, and 4 SR chests. Collect 8 chests to fuse a selectable SR drone (you pick which one instead of rolling). Finishing all 4 rounds gives you 23,200 diamonds, 3,200 chips, and 16 chests, enough to fuse two selectable SR drones.

Rules of thumb

  • Save all Rhenite between events. Don't buy drones outside the event window, you miss the event-point multiplier entirely.
  • Keep only the drone(s) you're actively building. Combination bonuses are permanent once earned: the game remembers the highest evolution each drone ever reached, so you can max -> sell -> max another drone and still accumulate the trio bonuses without keeping all drones in your roster.
  • Plan the spend before the event ends. Use the drone-event calculator at drone-calc-nu.vercel.app to work out how many rounds your current Rhenite and chip stockpile can cover.

Daily Diamond Baseline

The minimum diamond spend to not fall behind on progression:

  • Engine parts: 140💎
  • Chip factory parts: 100💎
  • Three daily challenge resources (AirSea Simulation for pilots, Critical Strike for drones, Sentinel Base for sentinels): 100💎 each, 300💎 total
  • Opportunistic daily shop buys: R pilot chests ~60💎, Alloy ~60💎, discounted Rhenite (≤90), discounted Recruitment Tickets (≤240)

You need to pull ~540💎/day from Collection Center just to break even on this baseline. Below that, you're slowly bleeding progression.

Pilot-Event Free Pulls

During pilot weeks you have more free-pull sources than most players realize: 2/day baseline + 3/day from Endless Voyage + 1 from daily missions. Daily discipline matters a lot here, completing daily missions and a full 10-level Endless Voyage run every day meaningfully compounds over months. The $4.99 pilot monthly pack and the Legion Warfare pass are the best paid value for badges + tickets, which is important because badges are typically the bottleneck resource for pilot progression (~10,000 badges = ~125 days of accumulation without events).

To hit the 300-pull reward tier without diamonds, you need ~225 tickets banked before the event starts. Below ~200 tickets banked, skip the event entirely, you won't hit a reward tier worth the diamond spend.

Sentinel Event: Every Other Month

Unlike other events, the sentinel event rewards purchases only (recasting, fusing, and leveling don't count toward event points). The event itself runs monthly, but the recommended participation cadence is every other month: skip one month to accumulate chips, then run the full event the next month to collect the free selectable SSR chest. Pushing the event every month tends to be wasteful because the ticket income doesn't keep up with the per-event ticket cost. Legion Clash ranking can also award free SSRs, which effectively lowers the real cost of an SSR well below the 38,000-ticket raw cost, ~34k with free refreshes, ~28k if you count Legion Clash drops.
